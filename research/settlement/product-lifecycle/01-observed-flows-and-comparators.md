# Observed material flows and legal comparators

[Product lifecycle](README.md)

The Global E-waste Monitor 2024 estimates that 62 billion kg of
electronic waste were generated in 2022 and about 22.3% were
documented as formally collected and recycled. This measures
documented formal activity using the report's definitions and
methods. The complement includes unverified or other pathways
and is not a direct estimate of illegal disposal. It supports
separating reported collection, actual treatment and material
outcomes. [ITU and UNITAR, Chapter 3](https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-d/opb/gen/D-GEN-E_WASTE.01-2024-PDF-E.pdf)

Article 5 of EU Directive 2024/1799 requires repair for goods
within its defined Annex II scope and limits unjustified
obstacles to repair, including some software or hardware
restrictions. It preserves the stated legitimate objective
justifications and legal conditions. It gives a concrete
comparator for safe independent access. It establishes no
universal support term, national skill stock or effect size.
[Directive (EU) 2024/1799, Article 5](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/1799/oj)

The Basel Convention secretariat records the changes effective
from 1 January 2025 for accepting parties, expanding the prior
informed consent scope for electronic waste. Applicability
depends on actual parties, classifications and laws. A genuine
used product and waste do not have the same legal treatment.
This proposal would require actual applicable permissions and
verified receiving. [Basel Convention e-waste amendments](https://www.basel.int/tabid/10103)

These sources motivate concrete comparisons. They do not price
the fixture, prove national recovery or validate the selected
initial support horizon. Local purchase, repair, acceptance and
treatment records would be needed.
