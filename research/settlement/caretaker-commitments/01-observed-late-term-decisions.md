# Two observed ways to handle decisions near a transition

The U.S. Government Accountability Office reviewed economically
significant final regulations in scoped presidential transition
and nontransition periods. It identified 67 final rules in the
last 120 days of the 2020–2021 transition, with 12 before or on
election day and 55 after election day. Removing the 11 rules
it identified as related to COVID-19 gives 56, with 10 before or
on election day and 46 after. This is publication timing, not a
finding about motive, urgency or the validity of a specific rule.

GAO reports 37 of 131 rules failed at least one examined timing or
submission requirement across the Trump administration periods it
reviewed. The denominator includes the examined nontransition
period. The observed split was 14 of 67 in transition and 23 of
64 outside it. More rules per week coincided with a lower share
of the rules failing the examined requirements in this case.
GAO found a rise in publication rates in prior transitions too
and distinguished procedure from the merits of the rules.
[GAO-23-105510, pages 15–16 and 24–27](https://www.gao.gov/assets/820/817228.pdf)

Australia's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet announced
on 28 March 2025 that the caretaker conventions were in effect
following dissolution, ahead of the election scheduled on 3 May.
Its guidance distinguishes ordinary administration from major
policy, appointments and contracts. For a major contract that
cannot be deferred, its options include consultation and exit
terms, subject to the governing circumstances. Its policy
guidance separates making a decision from announcing it and does
not treat the pre-dissolution period as a general caretaker period.
[Australian caretaker announcement](https://www.pmc.gov.au/news/federal-election-2025-caretaker-conventions-now-place)
· [Major decisions](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/guidance-caretaker-conventions/3-major-policy-decisions)
· [Major contracts](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/guidance-caretaker-conventions/5-major-contracts-or-undertakings)

The U.S. observations concern a presidential system and specific
rulemaking categories. The Australian sources describe conventions
and an actual trigger, without a measured outcome for one urgent
contract. They qualify different questions. Neither proves that
this country's proposed scope would prevent strategic binding or
that a shorter lawful option would always be available.
