# England. A lower absence average can hide a deeper loss

## Distinct measures and populations

For the 2024/25 academic year, England's Department for Education reports
overall absence of 6.78% of possible sessions, down from 7.15% in 2023/24.
Persistent absence, missing at least 10% of possible sessions, fell from
19.95% to 18.14% of pupil enrolments. Severe absence, missing at least
50%, rose from 2.30% to 2.39% of enrolments. The reported unit is an
enrolment with possible sessions in the relevant school census population.
State-funded alternative provision is reported separately, with overall
absence of 42.85% of possible sessions in 2024/25. None of these is a
measure of instruction received by all resident children.
[Department for Education full-year release](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/pupil-absence-in-schools-in-england/2024-25)

On the separate autumn 2025 census date, local authorities reported
34,700 children missing education. The population is of compulsory
school age, off school rolls and without suitable education elsewhere.
About 42% of this snapshot had been missing education for more than
12 weeks and the duration was unknown for 8%. Identification, local
recording and snapshots affect this measure. It cannot be joined to
the full-year absence percentage as a national count of learning lost.
A freely chosen and suitable home or other education is a different
situation. [Children missing education release](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/children-missing-education/2025-26-autumn-term)

## Observation and correction

Ofsted and the Care Quality Commission visited six local areas in 2025.
They found uneven oversight of shortened timetables and provision outside
school, and instances where access to health support depended on being in
school. They also observed stronger practice, including targeted school
support, staged alternative provision and links between hospital and
ordinary education. Visits, documents and respondent accounts give evidence
of both mechanisms. They do not estimate national frequency or the causal
effect of one institutional change.
[Ofsted and CQC thematic review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-experiences-of-children-with-send-who-are-not-in-school-a-thematic-review/beyond-the-classroom-the-experiences-of-children-with-send-who-are-not-in-school)

In one Ombudsman decision, the school had provided some part-time education
and actively tried to restore attendance before the council was aware.
The Ombudsman found fault after council awareness around April 2025 for
failing to consider its duty and the suitability of the materials offered.
The finding covers about one term to July. The council agreed to review
provision and pay £900. The published decision records the agreement and
request for compliance evidence. It gives no proof that the lost learning
was restored. The observed facts support earlier recognition and a named
owner without a single diagnostic gate. They do not show how common this
failure is. [Ombudsman decision 25 003 513](https://www.lgo.org.uk/decisions/education/alternative-provision/25-003-513)
