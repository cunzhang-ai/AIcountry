# The same housing notice and two different deadlines

*Design315 · Original legal comparators and a separate constructed case.*

Section 13 of Canada's Official Languages Act requires simultaneous
issuance in both official languages and equal authority for covered
instruments made bilingually. In *R. v. Daoust*, paragraphs 26–31,
the Supreme Court of Canada describes testing a shared meaning where
possible and checking it against legislative purpose and ordinary
interpretation. The case illustrates legal judgment rather than
automatic textual intersection. Its criminal-law result supplies
no housing rule here.

## Constructed deadline

Two official versions are designated for a housing review instrument.
One draft states that review may be requested through the tenth calendar
day after a notice is sent. The other states ten days after verified
receipt. These versions express different triggers. Notice is sent
on day 0 and received on day 2. A resident asks for review on day 11.
The dates and rule are authored. Other exceptions and interim powers
continue on their own law.

A single authoritative language simplifies conflict resolution.
People using another language can lose control over the final
wording even when invited to participate. Equally authentic
versions support common authority with real drafting, interpreting
and judicial costs. Making every rendering or model output
authoritative would create conflicting obligations without a
legitimate decision. Select authentic parity for the scope
lawfully designated, distinguish accessible nonbinding versions,
and retain meaningful participation in any other medium.
