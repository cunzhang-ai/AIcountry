# Governing health, education and long-term care

2 October 2026 · Supporting institutional decision for [country design 6](../COUNTRY-DESIGN.md), chiefly C03–C07/C13. Prepared by an AI research assistant. No affected public, professional body or independent reviewer participated. This selects a candidate for the working design, not enacted law, funded capacity or observed success.

## Decision and baseline

The current design guarantees effective access and names responsible authorities, but has not yet chosen a complete arrangement for financing, providing, regulating and replacing health care, basic education and long-term care. A claim against the state is hollow if every provider can close, select easier users or shift costs to an unpaid relative while no accountable institution maintains capacity. Conversely, concentrating all services in one ministry can make clinical judgment, teaching, family choice and opposition dependent on the government of the day. The decision is how to maintain universal public responsibility while distributing operational and professional power.

The baseline is the national guarantee-and-delivery schedule, national health authority, local education and care authorities, national fallback, plural economy and voluntary connected-access arrangement. Those commitments remain. The earlier three-year public scenario is authored arithmetic, not a workforce, geographic or provider feasibility study. No real provider list, population demand, assets or jurisdiction has been inspected.

## Coherent alternatives

| Package | Mechanism and attraction | Failure and power risk |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Predominantly direct national public provision**: one national service employs and owns most hospitals, schools and care services. | Can assign a clear universal duty, pool risk and move resources across regions; avoids reliance on for-profit provider selection. | One executive can control jobs, appointments and information across all three sectors. Central schedules may miss local conditions. Independent professional and user remedies are still needed. |
| **B. Entitlement vouchers and competing insurers/providers**: public money follows each eligible person, with regulator-set minimums. | Could make user choice meaningful where competing capacity actually exists; permits varied organizations. | Where staff, facilities or transport are scarce, a voucher buys no alternative. Risk selection, extra fees, fragmented pools and provider failures can turn nominal choice into exclusion. The state still needs a last-resort network. |
| **C. Selected candidate: public guarantee with sector-specific public capacity and plural supplemental delivery**: democratic law and pooled funding secure the floor; local/regional public networks maintain planned capacity; qualified nonprofit, cooperative and private providers may add capacity under equal-access terms. | Keeps a delivery counterparty and room for professional, local and user choice. Funding, accreditation, provider exit and rights review can be assigned to different offices. | Public networks can be patronized or too weak to replace contractors. Contracting and regulation consume scarce expertise; supplementation can drain workers from the public floor. |

Package C is a research choice, not a claim that mixed provision is generally best. It retains the earlier plural-economy choice but rejects the interpretation that a public guarantee can be satisfied by contracting alone. Package A becomes preferable if directly provided capacity can be governed independently and plural contracting adds cost or exclusion. Package B remains possible as a supplement where actual alternatives, equal access and continuity are demonstrated; it is rejected as the sole architecture for essentials. WHO's [pooling guidance](https://www.who.int/activities/pooling) supports avoiding fragmented health risk pools, while its [health-financing overview](https://www.who.int/health-topics/health-financing) separates funding, pooling and purchasing. Neither determines ownership of all services.

## Authority, financing and delivery

The **Assembly** enacts coverage, quality, nondiscrimination, funding and enforceable review, and can revise them through the democratic constitutional route. Ministers answer for policy and actual delivery, not individual diagnoses, marks or care preferences. Elected local authorities choose lawful organization. Regulators need statutory mandates, public appointment and removal criteria, separately reported funding, conflict disclosure and judicial/elected scrutiny. Calling an office independent does not establish independence if the same controller controls appointment, budget and enforcement. Staff and service users participate in design and complaints, but advisory representation does not replace electorate-wide lawmaking.

**Health:** Select one nationally pooled, compulsory and publicly accountable financing arrangement for the essential package, without employer-linked loss of cover. The national health authority purchases and maintains service capacity under appropriations; separate clinical-safety accreditation and rights review check its acts. Regional and local public clinical networks maintain planned primary, referral and urgent-care capacity. Accredited other providers can serve under equal-access contracts, but the national authority remains answerable for contractor failure. Clinicians retain independent case judgment within enacted law and professional standards. Patients retain consent, reasons, feasible provider choice and review. Capital, medicines, workforce, transport and emergency cover are visible costs. Universal pooling cannot imply unlimited care capacity.

**Basic education:** The national law provides free accessible basic education, a plural civic/curricular floor and protection against political indoctrination. National need-adjusted grants and equalization finance it; local elected education authorities run a viable public-school network, places and accessible transport. Building renewal and teacher development are separate from per-student operating money, so a small or high-need school is not made financially impossible by formula. Other lawful schools may exist; a publicly funded school must follow equal-access admission, fee and curriculum-floor rules. Teachers retain professional judgment within child-rights and curricular duties. Students and families have voice, reasons and appeal; school boards can inform governance but cannot displace elected budget authority. Inspection is separate from school management and local purchasing. UNESCO's [Convention against Discrimination in Education](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/convention-against-discrimination-education?hub=70211) is a comparator for free basic provision and educational choice, not a claim that AIcountry is a party; UNESCO's [non-state education guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/right-education/non-state-actors) makes clear why provision by others does not discharge state responsibility.

**Long-term care:** The Assembly defines the needs, autonomy, community-living, safeguarding and carer rights floor. National needs-based financing and equalization support local care authorities, which assess and directly provide or commission services. Local public or publicly controlled capacity is maintained for assessment, urgent replacement and support when a provider exits; qualified community, nonprofit, cooperative and private providers can add options. Recipient-directed budgets may enhance choice only with safe employment and continuity rules; they cannot become a cheap route to unsupported family work. Care recipients can choose among feasible lawful supports and use supported decision-making. Relatives have separate claims and can refuse unpaid labor. An independent rights and safeguarding body reviews neglect and coercive placement, including public services. The UN [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Article 19](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-19-living-independently-and-being-included-in-the-community.html) is a comparator for choice and community support, not a staffing or price formula.

These allocations answer different questions: funding follows solidarity; provision follows geography and professional practice; review follows rights and safety rather than the purchaser's interest in closing a case. Public contracts, aggregate waiting lists and inspection findings are available to elected scrutiny and opposition with personal privacy protected. Neither a contractor nor a professional guild is final judge of its own failure.

## Workforce, incentives and failure

The annual guarantee-and-delivery schedule must identify current and needed workers by service and region, training lead times, qualifications, supervision, retention, lawful migration, pay and conditions, substitute capacity, accessible facilities, materials and payment dates. One worker cannot simultaneously fill posts in multiple programs. Trainees are not qualified replacements. Workers retain organization, bargaining, safety and whistleblower protections. WHO's [health workforce strategy](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241511131), UNESCO's [teacher report](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/global-report-teachers-addressing-teacher-shortages-and-transforming-profession) and the ILO's [care resolution](https://www.ilo.org/resource/record-decisions/resolution-concerning-decent-work-and-care-economy) support examining training, employment and decent work as part of delivery. Their global findings do not supply counts for this hypothetical country.

Payment cannot rely on volume or enrolment alone. Health purchasing must account for complex need, prevention, continuity and referral without rewarding unnecessary treatment or rejection of expensive patients. School funding must account for high-need students and fixed small-site/transport costs without rewarding exclusion or test-score manipulation. Care contracts and recipient budgets must cover travel, supervision and safe labor rather than only contact minutes, while tracking autonomy and unmet need. Public providers face the same safety and access scrutiny. WHO's [strategic purchasing brief](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-UCH-HGF-PolicyBrief-19.6) explains why payment must align with need and legal entitlement; its [private-provider guidance](https://www.who.int/europe/publications/i/item/WHO-EURO-2025-11654-51426-78470) identifies risks of selection and unequal access. Exact formulas remain open until real costs and behavior can be assessed.

If a provider fails, the accountable sector authority must arrange transfer or interim service with lawful staff, facilities, transport, records and funds; a regulator addresses safety and a separate reviewer handles individual rights. Health continuity includes urgent stabilization and ongoing treatment; school continuity includes a place, teacher, transport and records; care continuity includes visits or safe alternative support without silently drafting relatives. The authority reports who was actually served, delayed or harmed and keeps unfulfilled claims open. If replacement capacity is absent, the cabinet must present a costed shortage package to the Assembly under existing rights and budget rules. A court can require lawful action and disclosure but cannot create a missing worker. A safety closure cannot become a shortcut for abandonment, and a provider contract cannot end the state's obligation.

## Transition, objections and status

No overnight nationalization or voucher conversion is assumed. Real adoption would first inventory law, providers, staff, collective agreements, assets, contracts, insurance liabilities, waiting lists and current patients/students/care recipients; map each duty and pending remedy to a successor; then cost training, duplicate operation and replacement capacity before changing payment or ownership. Ongoing treatment, school placement and care support continue through lawful transition. Providers and workers receive due process. National/local budgets reconcile transfers without double counting. Older AI-sovereign and sector clauses require legitimate fresh review; this paper does not amend them.

Material objections remain: the public backbone may crowd out useful providers or be captured by a ruling party; the regulator may share controllers with purchaser or guilds; a national health pool may centralize rationing; local school/care autonomy may preserve geographic inequality; recipient choice and professional safety can conflict; children's interests and family preferences may diverge. One satisfaction score cannot settle these conflicts. Revisit package C if real evidence shows no usable alternatives, worse staffing, systematic selection of low-need users or a failed remedy chain. More direct public provision remains a genuine rival.

**Evidence qualification:** cited WHO, UNESCO, ILO and UN materials support asking about pooling, workforce, equal education, provider incentives and autonomy; they do not establish which AIcountry arrangement works. **Research selection:** package C for this working design. **Legal authorization:** none. **Operational readiness:** no actual geography, workforce, cash or provider. **Observed outcomes:** none. No affected-party participation or independent review was obtained. The next bounded question is whether a real jurisdiction could staff, fund, independently review and lawfully transition packages A or C. A legitimate authority would compare them using current law, population need, providers, subannual cash, travel times and worker capacity, with patients, students, care recipients, carers and workers able to contest the frame.
