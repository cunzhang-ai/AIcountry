| Arrangement | Public value | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Retain ordinary formation, the budget and independent scrutiny | Allows bargaining and avoids a large reporting burden. The law and actual votes already control spending. | Earlier versions can be hard to compare. Voters may see individual promises that cannot be delivered together. |
| Optional independent costing with a reconciled public programme, selected | Preserves competition and confidential exploration. Makes whole-government choices and revisions reachable through existing votes. | A prestigious estimate can become a de facto endorsement. Scarce analyst time can favor established parties. |
| Require a certified complete manifesto and coalition package before polling or investiture | Could improve formal comparability. | Can exclude new challengers, grant model authors a veto and prolong caretaker control when estimates fail. |
| Bind the government legally to every campaign or coalition commitment | Makes literal continuity easy to demand. | Competing promises cannot always coexist. A broken forecast or revised public preference can turn into a legal impasse. |

Choose the second arrangement. The government's fiscal profile, qualified
capacity and authority need separate reasons. Keep current-law services,
ordinary parliamentary proposals and genuine programme revision available.
Qualified estimates are useful even when there is no credible single best
programme. Preserve material value disagreements rather than assigning a
government to the proposal with the lowest modelled cost.
