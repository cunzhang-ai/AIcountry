# C13. A nighttime interpersonal-safety failure

*Part of [C13 lived experience](../C13-lived-experience.md); integrated design 55 research, not enacted law.*

A resident's partner controls their phone, wages and home access and has made
a specific threat. It is late; a child needs care and an animal needs a safe
place. The resident reaches a clinic through a neighbor but does not want an
immediate police report. The first receiver must provide private clinical and
safety help, arrange a safe contact method and keep responsibility until a
qualified advocate, accepting shelter and lawful benefit receiver answer.
No single intake screen may expose the resident's location or count an
unanswered referral as safety. Time-sensitive medical care cannot wait for a
court form or forensic consent.

If immediate danger calls for an emergency restriction, police or a competent
court uses only the enacted ground, recorded evidence, minimal duration and
prompt review. The respondent receives safe notice and a real challenge;
the resident receives a reachable way to report a breach. A family court
separately decides the child's safe contact, while property, tenancy and
immigration claims keep their own files and deadlines. The safety plan must
work if the resident moves across a local boundary or later returns for help.

The failure test asks who answers after hours, who can pay for and accept a
safe place, who serves and enforces an order, who preserves evidence without
compulsory reporting, who can correct a wrongful order or leaked address,
and what happens if police, shelter and court share a captured controller.
Read the actual records and accounts of affected people, respondents,
children and workers before claiming success. A lower report count may
reflect fear; a higher order count may reflect real danger or overreach.
This invented case proves no operational capacity or superior outcome.
