# Parliament's right to ask and the route to usable evidence

## Canada. Protected inspection and later public release

On 10 December 2009 the House of Commons ordered the production of
documents relating to Afghan detainees. In April 2010 Speaker Milliken
found a prima facie question of privilege after the government failed
to comply. He allowed time for an accommodation. Three of four parties
agreed in June on a protected arrangement, and an ad hoc group of
parliamentarians began examining a collection of approximately 40,000
pages in July. The fourth party disputed the arrangement. Retired judges
served as arbiters. After dissolution in March 2011, the Minister tabled
approximately 362 documents totalling over 4,000 pages and the arbiters'
report in June 2011. The original collection, protected access and later
tabling are distinct scopes. The tabling count does not measure coverage
of the original order or actual protection for individual detainees.
[Speaker's ruling and official postscript](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/speakers-decisions/peter-milliken/ch01/decision18-e.html)

The arrangement is an example of separating protected parliamentary
inspection from public release. It also shows the importance of
agreement, time and coverage. The minister reported that release covered
all documents prioritized and sent to the arbiters for redaction
decisions, a narrower scope than the collection.
[Minister's tabling account](https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2011/06/minister-baird-tables-afghan-detainee-documents-reviewed-panel-arbiters.html)

## Australia. An order and an answer can still leave the question unresolved

In February 2020 the Australian Senate adopted a resolution requiring
answers about legal advice concerning the Income Compliance Program.
Its committee had rejected the minister's public-interest immunity
claim on the grounds presented. The committee later reported that
the responses it received about that advice continued to rely on the
rejected claim and that its April letter seeking clarification received
no response. Its findings are the committee's official account of this
dispute. They do not prove that every document was withheld forever
or establish how a different procedure would have changed the programme.
[Senate third interim report, chapter 1, paragraphs 1.4–1.12](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Community_Affairs/Centrelinkcompliance/Third_Interim_Report/section?id=committees%2Freportsen%2F024533%2F73662)

The later Royal Commission recommended recording why draft legal
advice remained unfinished and documenting important public service
decisions and discussions. Its recommendations are a later inquiry's
proposed responses, separate from the committee's production order and
from evidence of implementation.
[Royal Commission recommendations 19.5, 19.6 and 23.8](https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report)
