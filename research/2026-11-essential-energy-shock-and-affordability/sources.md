# Observed sources. UK energy affordability support

## Administrative and audit record

The UK National Audit Office's 2024 update records eight schemes for
domestic and non-domestic users over 2022–2024. It reported an estimated
total cost of £44 billion at the time of audit, with payments and
reconciliation still relevant to the estimate. The Energy Price Guarantee
subsidized unit prices. A separate bill support scheme paid automatically
for some meter and billing arrangements, while traditional prepaid
meters generally used vouchers that residents had to redeem. The audit
reports 98.7% successful delivery to the expected eligible customers
of that bill support scheme. For the application route for households
without a domestic electricity supply, it reports take-up of about
one-fifth of the department's provisional potential-eligibility
estimate. These have different denominators and selection processes.
Their difference cannot identify a causal effect of applying.
[Audit summary, paragraphs 3 and 8–9](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/energy-bills-support-an-update-summary.pdf)

## Subsequent impact evaluation

The UK energy department's commissioned 2025 evaluation reports barriers
for some vulnerable people using the application routes and finds that
higher-income households received more support in absolute terms. It
combines empirical records with modeled counterfactuals. In its lowest
income quartile for oil-heated homes, Table 4.4 estimates 100% above
its energy-burden threshold both with support and in the modeled
no-intervention case. The report notes that severity may still have
fallen. This is a modeled result for that group and threshold. It does
not measure safe indoor temperatures or exact individual receipt.
[Evaluation, executive summary and Table 4.4](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69257f399fd433badebc3102/domestic-energy-affordability-support-impact-economic-evaluation.pdf)
· [Archived original](original-sources/energy-evaluation.pdf)

Both sources describe the same historical intervention family and some
administrative inputs are shared. We infer that delivery must be tested
outside standard supplier billing and that subsidy cost, actual use and
household burden must be tracked independently. These sources supply
neither a physical energy shortage comparison nor evidence that the
proposed narrower bridge would outperform broad relief in all settings.
