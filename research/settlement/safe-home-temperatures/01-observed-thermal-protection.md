# Evidence for thermal protection and its limits

## A heat emergency reached people indoors

The British Columbia Coroners Service review identified 619 heat-related
deaths for 25 June through 1 July 2021. It reported that 98% occurred
indoors and noted delays between alerts and the response. This
supports testing whether a warning reaches people and leads to
protection inside actual occupied buildings. It does not estimate
which specific measure would have prevented how many deaths.
[Coroners Service death review](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/birth-adoption-death-marriage-and-divorce/deaths/coroners-service/death-review-panel/extreme_heat_death_review_panel_report.pdf).

## Retrofit can change both exposure and energy use

A New Zealand community cluster trial reported an estimated increase
of 0.5 °C in winter bedroom temperature after insulation, and energy
consumption in insulated homes at 81% of that in the control homes.
The study recruited 1,350 households in seven low-income communities,
with eligibility involving respiratory symptoms. It is evidence for
testing building improvements alongside energy provision. Its effect
size does not transfer to summer cooling, coincident peak demand,
different construction or an unidentified country.
[Original trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1808149/).

## A complaint and a completed repair are different outcomes

New York City's Comptroller examined how heat complaints reached
inspection and enforcement. The 2025 update reports continued low
conversion from complaints to formal violations and discusses multiple
explanations, including restoration, limited inspection capacity,
access barriers and the possibility of transient conditions during
inspection. Complaint counts alone do not establish thermal exposure
or the causal effect of a specific enforcement tool. TH therefore
keeps original receipt, independent evidence, urgent protection and
verified repair separate. [Comptroller's 2025 analysis](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/turn-up-the-heat-2025-update/).

## Standards need actual operating conditions

WHO's 2018 guidelines present 18 °C as a proposed general-population
cold-season reference for temperate or colder climates and discuss
greater needs for vulnerable groups. The high-temperature chapter
finds inadequate direct evidence for a universal 24 °C health
threshold and discusses local climate and humidity. These findings
inform the need for publicly reviewed local schedules and a faster
individual route. England's Approved Document O guidance for new
residential buildings illustrates the role of security, ventilation and
other usability limits in building compliance. It does not measure
protection received by people in existing housing.
[WHO cold and heat guidelines](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/276001/9789241550376-eng.pdf).
[England's overheating guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/approved-document-o-overheating-frequently-asked-questions).
